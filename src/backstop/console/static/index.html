One engine, two plans

The same sixteen rules, the same subjects, the same moments. Backstop's plan is the one being operated — what it permits goes to the scheduler and what needs a person goes to the queue. The naive plan is ruled beside it and dispatched nowhere: it is here because a good planner rarely proposes anything the rules must refuse, so a stream drawn only from Backstop's own playbook would show a boundary holding back nothing.

Four arms, one batch

Every arm faces identical orders and identical latent recoverability, fixed before any of them ran. The policy engine evaluates every action in every arm — the only difference is whether its rulings are obeyed, which is what makes the violation counts comparable rather than self-reported. Of which illegal is revenue taken by actions the rules refuse: a merchant cannot keep it, so crediting it would score the baseline for exactly the behaviour the engine exists to stop.

The three surfaces are tabled apart and never added. A recovered payment is one amount that landed; a re-registered mandate is a year of billing restored; a collected invoice is a balance already owed.

Which rules are load-bearing

Every rule is consulted on every action; none is skipped once another objects. So a rule that never speaks is not idle — it is a guarantee this batch never needed. The bar shows how often each one spoke to each plan.

What automation is not allowed to decide alone

An approval is permission from a person, not an exemption from the rules. Releasing re-runs all sixteen against the world that holds now, so a yes given on Tuesday can still be refused on Wednesday — and the reviewer is told which rule overtook them. Silence is not consent: an unanswered request expires, and the revenue is reported as given up by a staffing decision rather than quietly booked.

Held until its own moment, and ruled on again there

Three rules exist mainly to move an action rather than refuse it. That only means something if something honours the new time. Nothing fires early — there is no flush and no force — and the rules run again at fire time, because the world an action was judged in is not the world it lands in. A moment that passed while nothing was running is dropped as stale rather than fired a day late.

The bench

A fixed set of actions the engine must rule on the same way every time, each naming a real order, invoice or customer from this batch. Half of them are things recovery must never be allowed to do. Each carries the ruling it is expected to get, so a rule that quietly stopped denying something shows up here as a mismatch rather than as a line nobody read.

Finding the money, without a model

Segmented anomaly detection at three resolutions at once, rolled up to the scope each problem actually has. No LLM anywhere in it: this runs over every attempt in the batch and has to answer in seconds, and the money at risk is a number. Scored against the incidents that produced the batch, because a detector reporting six clusters is not evidence of anything until somebody says whether these are the right six.

Why it is failing

The one place on this page where a model decides anything. Detection says money is leaking and where; diagnosis says why, which is what chooses between a retry, a route switch, a re-engagement and doing nothing. Output is a closed enum so the engine downstream can switch on it, and every answer is printed beside the incident that actually caused the cluster.

The one component that reaches outside

Everything else on this page is a claim about what recovery would do. This dispatches, against Razorpay's test API, and it satisfies the same executor protocol the simulator does — so the ledger and the rules did not change to accommodate it. Nothing reaches it that the engine did not permit, the action is ruled on again at the moment you press the button, and what it sends is the action as the rules left it. Live keys are refused outright and there is no override; test mode really delivers, so notifications are off unless you ask.

And the one it lets in

A dispatch is not a recovery — a payment link is paid when a person opens it, hours later and out of band. The receiver takes the push rather than polling for it: raw bytes in, verdict out, signature checked against those bytes before anything parses them. It credits only what recovery itself dispatched, because a merchant's own payment links are paid all day and none of that is recovered revenue.

What happened, in order

Every approval, expiry, firing, deferral and refusal, newest first. A refusal names the rule that produced it — which is what makes “zero policy violations” a claim about a record rather than an assertion.
